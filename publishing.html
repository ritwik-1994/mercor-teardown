How Mercor takes a benchmark from idea to launch.

This walkthrough follows one Mercor benchmark from idea to live web page and beyond. Mercor never wrote its process down, so it's pieced together here from its website, its research papers and the dates its files went online.

What to say

Every Mercor benchmark goes through the same four stages, in the same order. First, decide where it sits in the catalog. Second, fill in a standard page. Third, pass a set of quality checks. Fourth, launch it and keep it updated.

Stage three matters most, because it's the reason AI labs trust Mercor's scores.

What to say

Mercor sorts its 44 benchmarks into four groups by who built them: its own, built with a partner, public benchmarks it added private tasks to, and other people's benchmarks it just runs.

The group decides how much the page shows. Mercor's own benchmarks get the most: error ranges on the scores, who wrote the tasks, how it was built, a release date and an FAQ. Other people's get the minimum. One gap: the group hints at who built a benchmark but never says it outright.

What to say

Every benchmark, even ones Mercor didn't build, gets the same 11-item information card. It covers who made it, how many tasks it has, how many times each task is run, how it's scored, what kind of input it uses, the task and question type, the test setup, how answers are graded, which AI does the grading, and the release date.

One rule covers them all: every model is tested at its strongest setting, and each task is run several times and averaged. Because the rule never changes, any two Mercor scoreboards can be compared fairly.

Part 62 min

12 quality checks, in four phases

What happens to every benchmark before it goes public.

Design

  • Decide what to test
  • Screen the experts: interview and paid trial

Build

  • Experts write tasks with model answers
  • Other experts review them
  • Throw out tasks that are too easy

Verify

  • Other experts re-solve a sample
  • Test the AI grader against humans
  • Run proper statistics

Release

  • Keep most tasks private
  • Set the license
  • Fill in the information card
  • Publish the test code
12 checksIn four phases: design, build, verify, release.
See it on Mercor's site
  • APEX-Agents research paperLook for section 3.5: 20% of tasks re-solved from scratch, 10% fixed; and the grader's 98.5% accuracy on 747 labels.
  • APEX-Accounting research paperLook for the 599 candidate tasks, the 97.1% grader agreement, and the 160-task private set.
What to say

Mercor's research papers describe the same 12 checks each time, in four phases.

Design: decide what to test, and screen the experts who'll write the tasks with an interview and a paid trial.

Build: experts write each task with a model answer, other experts review it, and tasks that AI models find too easy are thrown out.

Verify: other experts re-solve a sample from scratch, the AI grader is tested against human graders, and the results get proper statistics.

Release: keep most tasks private, set the license, fill in the information card, and publish the test code so others can repeat the results.

What to say

When a lab asks "Is this benchmark fair?", three numbers answer it. 20% of tasks were solved again from scratch by other experts, which shows the tasks can be solved. The AI grader agrees with human experts 98.5% of the time, which shows the marking is accurate. One benchmark started with 599 candidate tasks and kept only the hardest, which shows it's hard.

Two of Mercor's four launches ended with a best score around 25%: hard enough to be useful, not so hard it's hopeless.

What to say

Files are set up privately about a week before launch. Mercor launches every new benchmark the same way. The research paper goes online a day early, so there's something to cite. The next day the blog post, the scoreboard and the free data all go live together. News coverage follows a day later.

Mercor doesn't always hold to this. On two launches the free data came 45 and 71 days late. Benchmarks built by partners launch on the partner's own website first, and Mercor adds a scoreboard later. Only one, with Google, launched on both companies' sites on the same day.

What to say

Speed costs Mercor consistency. Its own pages disagree on basic facts: one says a benchmark came out in March, the paper says July. One says 100 tasks, the paper says 160. Old versions don't point to new ones, the free data is sometimes weeks late, and who built a benchmark is implied rather than stated.

Each of these would be easy to avoid with one trusted source for dates, task counts and versions.
