Mercor's leaderboards are a storefront. The free benchmark gets a lab's attention. The licensed dataset behind it is what they charge for.

Talk track

This is the business model on one slide. The public set is small, open and citable. It earns them the leaderboard and the papers.

Behind each public board sits a much bigger licensable set. For SWE-bench that's 12,000 tasks.

The public license says evaluation only. Training on it is forbidden. So a lab that wants the data has to buy it.

Talk track

Breadth gets them into every search. They run 25 benchmarks they didn't build, so a lab looking at SciCode or GPQA lands on Mercor.

Depth goes where they own the IP. Their own pages show uncertainty, who wrote the tasks and an FAQ. The third-party pages are thin.

Every page, thin or deep, ends in "Get in touch" and a link to buy data.

Talk track

This is the strongest page they have. Every frontier model scores mid-80s on the public test and mid-40s on fresh tasks.

A lab's RL lead reads that as: the public benchmark is contaminated, and Mercor has the uncontaminated version.

If asked "Is the drop real or just harder tasks?" We can't tell from outside. They don't publish a difficulty match between the two sets.

No public prices. The only contact route is a work-email form. A side business pays companies for de-identified workplace data (Slack, Gmail, Salesforce and 100+ tools) and licenses it to labs.

Talk track

Each step is a bigger commitment, and every step is on one page. A buyer always knows what the next step is.

The "same day" sample promise matters. It shortens the time from interest to a sales call.

061 minVR

"In partnership with" covers three different deals

Partner builds, Mercor hosts

Harvey LAB · FrontierCode · τ³-Banking

Harvey's lawyers wrote 1,749 tasks from real matters. Mercor runs the board. Cognition's and Sierra's own posts don't mention Mercor.

Co-built

Physical-Engineering's Last Exam

With Google Cloud AI Research. 85 tasks, 29 public. Same-day blogs from both companies on 2026-11-03.

Mercor builds, partner adds

APEX-Accounting · APEX-SWE

Built on Ramp's eval system, and on Cognition's view of what engineers expect. Mercor staff wrote both papers.

The partners are AI companies that each serve one industry (legal, fintech, coding, support), plus one frontier lab. Partners bring real workflows and a brand. Mercor brings experts, environments, runs, the board and the paper.

Talk track

Partners give them credibility in a domain they couldn't claim alone. A lawyer trusts a legal benchmark that Harvey wrote.

The Google one is the only true co-launch, and it's the most valuable kind.

Talk track

This is why labs believe their scores. They don't say "expert-verified" and stop. They say 20% of tasks were re-solved from scratch and 10% got fixed. They say the AI judge agrees with experts 98.5% of the time.

Two of their four launches landed in the 20–30% band, hard enough to matter but not hopeless.

Versioning is loose. A new version gets a new Hugging Face repo, and the blog post acts as the changelog. Old repos don't point to new ones. Open data sometimes ships 6–10 weeks late.

Talk track

The paper goes up a day early so the launch has something to cite. Then everything ships on the same day, and press follows.

The weak parts are versioning and late data. Old versions don't point to new ones, and open data sometimes ships weeks later.

101 minV

Where they're weak

Dates disagree. APEX-Accounting "released March 2026" on one page; paper and blog are July.
Task counts disagree. 100 on the board, 160 in the paper.
No changelog. Old repos don't point to new versions.
Thin pages. 35 of 43 have no uncertainty, FAQ or date.
No quality numbers in the data catalog. The rigor stays in papers.
License terms not public.
Talk track

They move fast, and it shows. Their own pages contradict each other on dates, task counts and even their expert network: 5M+ on one page, 4M+ on another.

A lab doing diligence notices that. A single source for dates and counts would fix most of it.
