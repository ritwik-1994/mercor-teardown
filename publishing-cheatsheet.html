How Mercor publishes a benchmark, in plain words

This walkthrough follows one Mercor benchmark from idea to live web page and beyond. Mercor never wrote its process down, so it's pieced together here from its website, its research papers and the dates its files went online.

Each card below matches one part of the walkthrough, in the same order. Read the card, say it in your own words, and land the one thing to remember.

Part by part

Part 11 min

The four stages

Every Mercor benchmark goes through the same four stages, in the same order. First, decide where it sits in the catalog. Second, fill in a standard page. Third, pass a set of quality checks. Fourth, launch it and keep it updated.

Stage three matters most, because it's the reason AI labs trust Mercor's scores.

Part 21 min

Why Mercor publishes at all

Publishing is Mercor's marketing. A free benchmark gets attention and gets cited in research. The money comes from selling the much larger set of tasks behind it to AI labs for training.

So Mercor shows enough to be trusted and keeps most of the data back.

Part 32 min

Stage 1: where the benchmark sits

Mercor sorts its 44 benchmarks into four groups by who built them: its own, built with a partner, public benchmarks it added private tasks to, and other people's benchmarks it just runs.

The group decides how much the page shows. Mercor's own benchmarks get the most: error ranges on the scores, who wrote the tasks, how it was built, a release date and an FAQ. Other people's get the minimum. One gap: the group hints at who built a benchmark but never says it outright.

Part 42 min

Stage 2: the standard information card

Every benchmark, even ones Mercor didn't build, gets the same 11-item information card. It covers who made it, how many tasks it has, how many times each task is run, how it's scored, what kind of input it uses, the task and question type, the test setup, how answers are graded, which AI does the grading, and the release date.

One rule covers them all: every model is tested at its strongest setting, and each task is run several times and averaged. Because the rule never changes, any two Mercor scoreboards can be compared fairly.

Part 51 min

Stage 2: the two-score page

When Mercor extends someone else's public benchmark with its own private tasks, the page always shows two scores per model: one on the public tasks and one on Mercor's fresh ones.

Top models score about 84% on the public version and about 46% on the fresh tasks. That single chart makes the case for buying fresh data, with no sales copy needed.

Part 62 min

Stage 3: the 12 quality checks

Mercor's research papers describe the same 12 checks each time, in four phases.

Design: decide what to test, and screen the experts who'll write the tasks with an interview and a paid trial.

Build: experts write each task with a model answer, other experts review it, and tasks that AI models find too easy are thrown out.

Verify: other experts re-solve a sample from scratch, the AI grader is tested against human graders, and the results get proper statistics.

Release: keep most tasks private, set the license, fill in the information card, and publish the test code so others can repeat the results.

Part 71 min

Stage 3: the three numbers that earn trust

When a lab asks "Is this benchmark fair?", three numbers answer it. 20% of tasks were solved again from scratch by other experts, which shows the tasks can be solved. The AI grader agrees with human experts 98.5% of the time, which shows the marking is accurate. One benchmark started with 599 candidate tasks and kept only the hardest, which shows it's hard.

Two of Mercor's four launches ended with a best score around 25%: hard enough to be useful, not so hard it's hopeless.

Part 81 min

Stage 4: what every launch includes

Every Mercor launch includes four things: a research paper, a blog post with named authors, a scoreboard, and a private set of test tasks. Most launches also include a small free sample of tasks and the code to run them.

None of them include anything to track versions, such as a list of what changed.

Part 92 min

Stage 4: the launch timing

Files are set up privately about a week before launch. Mercor launches every new benchmark the same way. The research paper goes online a day early, so there's something to cite. The next day the blog post, the scoreboard and the free data all go live together. News coverage follows a day later.

Mercor doesn't always hold to this. On two launches the free data came 45 and 71 days late. Benchmarks built by partners launch on the partner's own website first, and Mercor adds a scoreboard later. Only one, with Google, launched on both companies' sites on the same day.

Part 101 min

Stage 4: after launch

After launch Mercor is fast but untidy. Fast: new AI models appear on its scoreboards within days of coming out.

Untidy: a new version gets a brand-new download page, the old page isn't updated to point to it, and scores on the board are swapped out with no record of what changed.

Part 111 min

Where the process breaks

Speed costs Mercor consistency. Its own pages disagree on basic facts: one says a benchmark came out in March, the paper says July. One says 100 tasks, the paper says 160. Old versions don't point to new ones, the free data is sometimes weeks late, and who built a benchmark is implied rather than stated.

Each of these would be easy to avoid with one trusted source for dates, task counts and versions.

Words you'll hear

Benchmark
A test for AI models: a fixed set of tasks, with a score for how many each model gets right.
Training data
Example tasks with correct answers that AI labs use to teach their models. This is what Mercor sells.
Leaderboard
The public scoreboard for a benchmark, ranking models by score.
Private (held-out) tasks
Tasks kept secret so no model can have seen them during training. Scores on them are more honest.
Contamination
When test tasks leak into a model's training data. The model then scores higher than it should, like a student who saw the exam.
Score (pass@1)
The share of tasks a model gets right on the first try. Mercor runs each task a few times and averages the result.
Error range (±)
How much a score could move by chance. If two models are closer than this, neither is really ahead.
Headroom
How much room models still have to improve. A benchmark where the best model scores 20 to 30% is hard enough to be useful but not hopeless.
AI grader
An AI model that marks answers against a checklist, instead of a human doing it. It's only trusted if it agrees with human experts.
Evaluation-only license
You may use the free tasks to test a model, but not to train one. Training data has to be bought.
arXiv
The free website where AI research papers are posted.
Hugging Face
The main website where AI datasets and models are shared and downloaded.
APEX
Mercor's own benchmark brand, for example APEX-Agents and APEX-Accounting.
Information card
Mercor's standard 11-item summary for each benchmark, on its methodology page.
Embargo
Journalists get the story early but agree not to publish before launch day.
Changelog
A list of what changed between versions. Mercor doesn't keep one.

Questions you might get

Does Mercor publish its checklist?

No. We pieced it together from six of its research papers, which describe the same checks each time, plus the dates its files went online.

How long does a launch take?

About a week from the first files going up privately to the full launch.

Does Mercor always release free data?

On four of its five launches. Twice it arrived 45 and 71 days late.

How does Mercor handle new versions?

It creates a new download page and uses a blog post to explain the changes. The old page isn't updated, and scores on the board are simply replaced.

What can't we see from outside?

Who signs off a launch, how many reviewers each task gets, how many tasks are thrown out, and what Mercor charges.
